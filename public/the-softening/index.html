The points

Nine places on the body where the practice rests its fingers. Traditions map them to meridian endpoints. You can hold that map lightly and still notice what each one tends to carry. Tap any point, on the figure or in the list, and a close-up opens showing exactly where it sits.

Side of hand

The setup point, tapped while you name what is true. It meets the part of you that is not sure it wants to let this go yet. That part is allowed to be here.

Top of head

Scattered thinking, the feeling of being unmoored or spread too thin across the day.

Eyebrow

Grief and the beginning of tears. The place sadness gathers before it has words.

Side of eye

Anger and frustration, especially the kind held tight behind a calm face.

Under eye

Raw anxiety. The stomach-flutter fear that arrives before thought does.

Under nose

Shame and embarrassment. What you hope nobody saw.

Chin

Self-doubt, and confusion about what you are allowed to want or deserve.

Collarbone

The deep worry. The bracing you have been doing so long it feels like posture.

Under arm

Self-worth. The old ache of not enough, resting quietly along the ribs.

Tap lightly, five to seven times per point, with two fingers. Pressure is not the medicine. Attention is.

Why it works

A short and honest account, without overclaiming.

Tapping, or EFT, pairs two simple things: light touch on a set of acupressure points, and saying the true thing out loud. The points come from the meridian maps of Chinese medicine. The saying comes from something psychology has known for a long time: what gets named and felt can move, and what stays vague stays stuck.

What the research actually shows

The evidence base is young and imperfect, but it is consistent enough to take seriously. Randomized trials have measured meaningful drops in cortisol, the body's main stress hormone, after a single hour of tapping. Meta-analyses of trials on anxiety and depression report moderate to large effects, with the honest caveat that many studies are small. Brain imaging work on cravings and fear responses shows calming shifts in the regions that hold alarm.

The most credible explanation is not magic and does not need to be. Tapping looks like a form of exposure: you deliberately touch the painful thought while giving the body a steady, rhythmic signal of safety. The amygdala, the part of the brain that keeps the alarm ringing, appears to take that signal seriously. The alarm quiets, and the thought and the panic slowly uncouple.

Why the body, and not just the mind

You have probably already understood your pattern. Most thoughtful people have. Insight lives in the thinking brain, but the flinch lives lower down, in the systems that decide what is dangerous before you get a vote. That is why you can know something is safe and still brace. Tapping works at the level of the brace. It does not argue with the fear. It sits with it until the body stops treating it as an emergency.

What this practice is honest about

The scripts here are not affirmations. Repeating a bright sentence you do not believe tends to tighten the body, not soften it. So the scripts stay in what is true for most of their length, and only soften at the end, tentatively, the way real softening happens.

A note on care. Tapping is a self-help practice, not a substitute for mental health care. If you are working with trauma, crisis, or a clinical condition, this practice can sit alongside professional support, not in place of it. If something surfaces that feels too big to hold alone, that is not a failure of yours or of the practice. It is a sign to bring in a trauma-informed therapist or counselor. And if you are in crisis right now, please reach out to your local emergency number or crisis line, or to someone you trust. You are allowed to be helped.
